The Empirical Proof — What the Banks Admitted

For most of the twentieth century, three theories of banking competed for the mantle of truth. Only one of them described what actually happens.Layer 1

Theory 1 · Intermediation

Banks take in deposits and lend them out. They are middlemen between savers and borrowers.

Theory 2 · Fractional Reserve

Banks pool deposits and lend a multiple of them, holding a fraction in reserve. The money multiplier.

Theory 3 · Credit Creation

Banks create credit out of nothing when they issue a loan. The deposit is created by the act of lending. No pre-existing money is required.

For decades, the textbooks taught Theory 1. The regulators operated as if Theory 2 were true. In 2014, two things happened that should have rewritten every economics syllabus in the world.

The Mechanism — Productive vs. Speculative Credit

Werner's work provides the missing engine for what looks like a paradox.Layer 2

When banks create credit, they do not create it for one purpose. They create it for many. Some credit finances productive activity — a factory, a farm, a business that produces goods or services and generates income that can repay the loan out of the income it creates. Werner calls this C_R — credit for real production.

Other credit finances speculation — the purchase of existing assets at ever-higher prices, in the expectation of resale. The loan is not repaid out of productive income. It is repaid out of the next buyer's loan. Werner calls this C_F — credit for financial speculation.Layer 1

C_R → credit for real production → income generates repayment
C_F → credit for financial speculation → next buyer's loan generates repayment

Banking systems dominated by C_R grow the real economy.
Banking systems dominated by C_F inflate asset prices.

When the banking system's output shifts from C_R to C_F — which is what happened across the Western world between 1971 and now — the real economy can stagnate while asset prices reach all-time highs. That is not a paradox. It is the predictable output of a credit-creation system that has been redirected toward speculation.

Wall Street is not strong despite the political chaos. It is strong because the credit that should have financed production is instead financing the acquisition of existing assets — and the political chaos provides the Wall of Worry that keeps capital flowing to the same institutional holders.
