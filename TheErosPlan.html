How to Make a Scene

One formula. Three inputs. Any scene, in ten minutes.

The Scene Formula. Pick a postulate. Name its hidden twin. Place a character at one of four conditions. Introduce an opponent running the opposing postulate. Apply one of eight overwhelm classes. End the scene when the character freezes (TIPM) or shifts (resolution).

Three beats: Setup (postulate named) / Conflict (hidden twin revealed) / Resolution (freeze or shift).

Worked example

Postulate: A parent must protect their child from every danger.

Hidden twin: A parent must never admit they cannot protect.

Condition: To Be Known (the parent needs recognition as a good parent).

Opponent: A child who needs the parent to admit uncertainty.

Overwhelm class: Prevented from Knowing (the parent cannot learn what the child needs because the child's confession would expose the parent's failure).

Setup: The parent stands in the doorway. The child returns with a small wound from a fight at school. The parent's first response is not concern — it is reflexive anger at the school. The postulate shows itself.

Conflict: The child begins to say something honest. The parent cuts them off. Not by shouting — by offering a solution the child didn't ask for. The hidden twin does its work. The parent cannot admit they cannot protect, so every honest moment from the child is quietly redirected before it can land.

Resolution (freeze): The child goes silent. The parent reads the silence as agreement. The scene ends with the parent feeling successful. The child has learned not to speak. The freeze has landed.

Resolution (shift): The parent notices the child's silence and pauses. Instead of another solution, they say: "I don't actually know how to fix this. Tell me what you need." The child does not answer immediately. The scene ends. The shift has begun.

Appendix Z The Scene Formula — three beats, three worked examples ›

Pick a postulate. Name its hidden twin. Place a character at one of four conditions. Introduce an opponent running the opposing postulate. Apply one of eight overwhelm classes. End the scene when the character freezes (TIPM) or shifts (resolution).

The three beats

  1. Setup — The postulate shows itself. A scene where the postulate is visible in the character's behaviour. They may not know they hold it. The audience does.
  2. Conflict — The hidden twin surfaces. Something happens that would require the character to admit the twin. They either refuse (freeze incoming) or notice (shift incoming).
  3. Resolution — Freeze or shift. The scene ends in one of two states. Freeze: the character reinforces the game. Shift: the character sees the twin and lets one thing go.

Worked example 2

Postulate: A leader must never show weakness.

Hidden twin: A leader must never be seen as someone who needs the team.

Condition: To Be Known (needs to be seen as competent).

Opponent: A team member who needs the leader to admit they are human.

Overwhelm class: Preventing from Being Known (the leader prevents themselves from being known as human to preserve authority).

Setup: The leader arrives early, as always. Reviews the plan once more, alone. Refuses every offer of help with a smile.

Conflict: The team member stays late, finds the leader visibly struggling with a decision. Offers to help. The leader brushes it off with a joke. The joke is warm, but it is also a wall.

Resolution (freeze): The team member leaves. The leader stays late again, alone, working on the thing they will not delegate. Nothing changes. Tomorrow, the same scene repeats.

Resolution (shift): The team member does not leave. They pull up a chair. They say nothing. They just sit there. Eventually the leader says, quietly: "I don't know which way this goes." The scene ends. The shift has begun.

Worked example 3

Postulate: A child must not be a burden to their aging parent.

Hidden twin: A child must never admit the parent is aging.

Condition: Not To Be Known (hides need).

Opponent: The parent, who needs the child to allow them to still give.

Overwhelm class: Forced to Be Known (the parent's aging forces the child into the open).

Setup: The child visits on Sunday. Brings groceries. Does not sit down. Cleans the kitchen while talking. Does not notice the parent watching the child move too fast.

Conflict: The parent tries to make tea — a small thing they can still do. The child intercepts. "Let me get that." The parent's hand stops mid-reach. Both pretend it was nothing.

Resolution (freeze): The child leaves. The parent sits down, quietly, and does not make tea. The Sunday ritual continues unchanged. The child believes the visit went well.

Resolution (shift): The child notices the pause in the parent's hand. They step back and say: "Actually, will you make it? Yours is better." The parent makes the tea. Nothing is said about aging. Everything is said about being allowed to still give.

Appendix AB The Iteration Clock ›

A single reference diagram, rendered as text. Time = count of iterations. Not a tunnel. Not a container. A count.

Iteration sequence:

Moment n ────→ Moment n+1 ────→ Moment n+2 ────→ Moment n+3
↓ ↓ ↓
completed completed completed
iteration iteration iteration

Time = n where n is the count of completed iterations

Now = the iteration currently executing
Past = iterations that froze before completing (frozen TIPM)
Future = iterations not yet initiated

Frozen structure = an iteration that stopped. Time does not advance in its region.
Coherent structure = iterations complete rapidly. Time "moves" fast.
Incoherent structure = iterations stall, repeat, or cycle. Time drags.

How to apply the iteration clock

  • In a script: every scene is either an iteration completing or stalling. A stalled iteration is a scene that repeats. A completing iteration is a scene that advances.
  • In a game: iteration rate is the game's heartbeat. Coherent regions have faster animations. Frozen regions have still ones.
  • In a comic: the panel count per action. Coherent panels are single-frame. Frozen panels are repeated identical frames.
  • In a score: iteration rate is tempo. Coherent passages move quickly. Frozen passages hold, sustain, do not resolve.
Appendix AC The Coherency Meter ›

Same energy, different coherence, different outcome. The difference between a 5W bulb and a 5W laser is not power. It is coherency.

Bulb (incoherent):
• 5 watts of energy
• Scattered in 360°
• Illuminates a room
• Cannot cut through fog

Laser (coherent):
• 5 watts of energy
• Focused in one direction
• Cuts through steel
• Travels across a room, intact

Formula: C = f / (f + d)
where f = focused energy, d = dispersed energy

Result: Coherence is not about more energy. It is about less dispersion.

Application to the character

Same character, same talent, same circumstances — different coherence.

  • Incoherent: The character's attention is scattered. They help everyone. They say yes to everything. Their work is diffuse. Nothing lands.
  • Coherent: The character's attention is focused. They help the people they can actually help. They say yes to the few things that matter. Their work concentrates. Everything lands.

The scene beat: The moment a character becomes coherent is when they stop trying to do everything and start doing the one thing. This is often the moment of the film's central action.

The gameplay beat: The player's coherency meter rises when they choose fewer targets, focus more. Coherency is a resource in the game — spend it on the wrong things and it scatters. Spend it on the right things and it cuts.
