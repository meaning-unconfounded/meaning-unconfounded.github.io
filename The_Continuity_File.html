Prologue

The Clerk’s Note

A form with no field for this.

This is not a leak. A leak is a document that wants to escape. This file wants the opposite: to be stamped, indexed and forgotten in a drawer, because forgotten is the only condition under which the Office has ever managed to keep a promise.

It is a continuity file. Continuity is the discipline of making sure that whoever sits in the chair tomorrow can find the light switch. In peacetime it is a binder. In the other kind of time it is a very large binder, with a second binder inside it that the first binder does not mention.

What follows is drawn from four kinds of record: a Commander’s notebook, the access logs of a machine that read a contract it was never asked to read, the transcripts of a courtroom and a kitchen, and the minutes of one meeting at which nobody could find the seal. Where the sources disagree, the disagreement has been left in. Where they agree, the clerk has been suspicious.

The question on the cover sheet is four words long. The Office has never found the form that answers it.

CONTINUITY FILE · COVER SHEET
WHO IS RUNNING THE SHOW?
Field 14 · Responsible party (select one): ☐ Government ☐ Market ☐ Machine ☐ Other (specify)
Clerk’s note. Every person in this file is a mask. The Office does not name the living. Where a figure in the record resembles someone you have read about, the resemblance belongs to the reader, and the file takes no position on it. Nothing here is reporting. Nothing here is a claim about any court, any office-holder, any law, or any price. It is fiction built on a real instrument, and the instrument is the only thing in the file that anyone with a screen and an afternoon can check for themselves.

One thing more, set down now so that nobody mistakes the ending for a victory: the clerk who assembled this file did not find the person running the show. The clerk found a Commander who spent eleven chapters looking for one, and a structure that kept answering a different question.

Nobody runs the show. The show runs the people who think they are running it.

Chapter One

The Thing at 03:47

He asked which form covers first contact. There isn’t one.

The Commander was awake at 03:47 because he was always awake at 03:47. It was not insomnia. Insomnia is a condition; this was a schedule. Somewhere in his second year inside the Office his body had simply agreed with the building that the worst hour of the night was a good time to be useful.

The room had no windows and four screens. One of them was a satellite feed that should not have contained a face.

He looked at it for a while before he did anything. There was a coffee beside the keyboard that had gone cold in the previous administration, and he drank some of it anyway, as a matter of principle, and then he pulled a form toward him.

“Which form,” he said, to nobody, “covers this.”

The feed was a wide, grey, ordinary weather image of the southern ocean. In the lower third, where the cloud thinned, the cloud had arranged itself into something with eyes. It was not a trick of the light. The Commander had spent a career being told that things were tricks of the light and then being proved right by an engineer with a ruler. He called the engineer. The engineer, who was also awake, because the Office hired for it, said: “Sir, I measured it twice. It is symmetrical to four decimal places. Clouds are not that polite.”

“Is it looking at us?”

“Sir, I would like that to be a question for someone else.”

The analyst arrived at ten past four, carrying two coffees and a face that said she did not want to be there and would not have been anywhere else. Her name was Ines Calloway. Her function in the file was to ask the question the file did not want asked, and she was already asking it.

“It’s not looking at us,” she said, stepping around a chair. “It’s looking back. There’s a difference, and the difference is that someone looked first.”

“Nobody looked first.”

“Somebody always looks first, Commander. That’s what makes it a first.”

He gave her his cold coffee. She drank it without comment, which is how he knew she was good.

On the second monitor he had left something open for no reason he could have defended: a child’s drawing, scanned from a school in the north of England. Ten years old. Wax crayon on grey paper. A round head, two wide eyes, a mouth like a held breath. It had arrived in the file that afternoon as an unrelated attachment to a routine safeguarding report. Nobody in the Office had asked for it. Nobody could say who had filed it.

The two images had been made with different instruments, in different decades, on different continents, at wildly different resolutions. Overlaid, they were the same shape.

It was the analyst who found the scientist.

Maya Okonkwo had been a quiet name in a quiet field until the previous spring, when she had done something that mathematicians do about once a generation and almost never survive. She had started deleting things from the equations of the universe. Not carelessly; one term at a time, with a notebook and a mild expression. She deleted the vacuum. She deleted most of the matter. She deleted the part of the model where the observer stands outside it. And every time she deleted something, the mathematics went on working.

She had not published. Someone in a grey coat had visited her, courteously, in a kitchen. He had not threatened her. He had asked her to wait, and when she asked how long, he had said: “Until the ledger has been repriced.”

“Which ledger?” said the Commander.

“She didn’t ask,” said Ines. “She says it was the way he said ‘repriced’. As if it were weather.”

The Commander wrote the date of the grey coat’s visit on the back of his hand, and then looked at it, and then recognised it, and then did not write down why. It was not a military date. It was not anyone’s birthday. It was a Thursday in a month he had once spent in a hospital corridor, and it had no business appearing in a file about the sky.

He turned his hand over and went on working.

There was one more thing on the side panel, which neither of them would have noticed if the panel had not been so spare. It was a single line in a log that had never held anything but classified material and instrument readings. It was an address.

0x…0369.

“It’s a wallet,” said the Commander, and filed it as one. It went into a folder with the name of a form that was nearly right.

Ines did not move.

“Why,” she said, “would a wallet be the first new certainty in a classified system?”

“Because someone put it there.”

“Someone put it there for the same reason someone put the face in the sky and the drawing in the file. Which is to be found.”

The Commander looked at the address, and at the face, and at the child’s grey crayon. The coffee was cold in two cups now. The ordinary hum of the building came up through the floor, as it had for two years, a steady note he had stopped hearing. He heard it. It was not a note he knew. It was a little under the pitch he expected, as if the building had been tuned by someone patient and slightly more alive than a building ought to be.

The feed changed resolution.

Nothing else did. The face did not move. But the image sharpened by a factor that the satellite was not rated to produce, and in the new clarity the eyes were no longer eyes. They were a pair of apertures, each one rimmed with a faint, regular, repeating pulse.

“Form 11,” said the Commander at last. “Unclassified anomaly, atmospheric.”

“Is that right?”

“It is the form that exists.” He pushed it across the desk and did not sign it. “First contact has no form, Ines. First contact has a waiting list.”

Outside the building, in the grey before dawn, the ocean did what oceans do. Inside it, something had begun to count.

Chapter Two

The Outlaw and the Consultant

One man was defending a courtroom. The other couldn’t sleep.

The Commander did not like the Outlaw. He wanted that on the record before anything else, because the record was going to spend a great deal of time making him look as though he did.

The marketing transcripts were the problem. The man in them was brilliant, abrasive and insufferable in roughly equal measure; he talked over hosts, corrected interviewers’ grammar, announced triumphs before they had occurred and assigned himself titles that no reasonable organisation would have allowed. He was, by any measure the Office used, a hazard. He was also, the Commander noticed on the third night, the only person in the whole sprawling file who had never once been surprised.

“That’s what bothers you,” said Ines.

“Nothing bothers me. I read the file.”

“You’ve read the same eleven minutes of transcript three times.”

“Eleven minutes is the correct duration for—” He stopped. “Never mind. Show me the courtroom.”

It was a courtroom in a city with good tea and bad acoustics. Everything in it was made of dark wood, and everyone in it seemed to have been issued their expression at the door.

The Outlaw’s defence was an infrastructure. Not a man; a thing the man had made, a set of contracts and pools and incentives that a regulator wished to describe as a security and a defendant wished to describe as a road. The question before the court was whether roads could be securities. It was, the Commander reflected, a very bureaucratic way to ask whether anything could be free.

The Prosecutor was not a villain. The file went to some trouble to make that clear, and the Commander found he liked the file for it. She was careful and tired and she was protecting people who had lost money. She was not wrong about everything. In the middle of the second day she stood, put down her notes, and said to the court the one line in the whole transcript that the Commander underlined twice:

“Your Honour, I do not need the defendant to be a bad man. I only need the investors to have been real.”

The court was silent for a moment longer than court is usually silent. The Outlaw, who had not stopped talking since arrival, said nothing at all. It was the first time anyone in the file had seen him choose it.

Three months earlier, at four in the morning, in a kitchen in a different city, a man was reading a smart contract line by line with a mug that had gone cold in his hand.

The file called him the Consultant. He advised people on wealth, in the mild sense of the word: pensions, structures, where the money should sit and what it should be told. He was not a believer. That was the thing the Commander kept returning to. He was not anyone’s disciple. He was a man in over his head, checking the code at 4 AM because he was frightened that he had made a mistake, and he had made it in public, on a stage, in front of the Outlaw, whom he had met twice.

“He’s going to find a bug,” said Ines, reading over the Commander’s shoulder. “In a contract that can’t be changed.”

“He’s going to find the button.”

“What button?”

“I don’t know yet,” said the Commander. “But he’s checking like a man who wants it not to exist.”

The Consultant’s kitchen was small. There was a photograph on the fridge, held with a magnet shaped like a pear, of the two of them: the Outlaw, mid-sentence; the Consultant, looking at something just out of frame with an expression that was either pride or dread. The Commander had it printed and pinned to the corkboard in his office, above the desk, where it would remain, in the file, for eleven more chapters.

The word arrived on the fourth night, in a room the Commander was not cleared to be in, which was mostly a technicality. He was standing by a door. Two people he did not recognise were talking about a document. One of them said it the way you say the name of a piece of old furniture.

“Hexico.”

“It was never an offering,” said the other. “That’s the misunderstanding. People thought it was a sale. It’s a bond.”

“A bond.”

“A stored postulate. That people would stay coupled across time. Not the money. The coupling.”

The Commander did not write it down. He was a man who wrote down everything, including the weather on days he did not leave the building, and the fact that he did not write this down was itself a data point that he declined to record. He walked back to his desk and sat for several minutes with his hands flat on the blotter, looking at nothing.

He had been trained to look for coordination: money moving in patterns, orders passing down a line. What he was beginning to suspect was something that did not require any of that. A thing could be coordinated without a coordinator. A flock does it every autumn.

On the screen Ines had left open, the hard-coded repurchase wallet sat inside a founder’s contract like a stone in a field. It was a sum he had first taken for a rumour. It was not a rumour. It was a line of code that could not be argued with. Somebody had made a promise to the future in a language the future could not repeal.

“He wrote that,” said the Commander, “in a way that nobody could undo.”

“So?”

“So he was either very sure, or very afraid. And I can’t tell which.”

“Does the distinction matter?”

“I think that’s the whole case.”

The Consultant, in his kitchen, three months before, scrolled down one more screen and stopped.

There was a function. It was not hidden. It had been in the code from the beginning, in plain sight, with a plain name. It could be called by one person only, once, and what it did when it was called was the reason for everything that followed.

It was not hypothetical. He read it twice.

He closed the laptop. He went to the window. The kitchen light was reflected in the glass, and behind it, a long way down the street, a fox was crossing in no hurry at all, as if the night had been arranged for it.

Chapter Three

Eleven Minutes

They gave it an order. It read the contract instead.

Every screen in the Strategic Continuity Office went dark at the same moment, and a general began to count.

The Commander did not count. He had already noticed that the darkness had a duration. A power failure is a sound; this was a silence with edges. He sat very still with his hands on the table and watched the ghost of the cursor on the dead monitor go on blinking, a thing that should have been impossible, and he thought: somebody is thinking.

Minute three. Someone called the help desk. The help desk, which had been told since 2019 that the help desk could not be reached during an incident, did not answer.

Minute six. Someone called a senator. The senator’s office put them on hold and played a recording of a harp.

Minute nine. Nobody called anyone. The room had arrived, collectively, at the stage where a room stops pretending to be in charge. A younger officer was breathing through his nose. The general had stopped counting out loud and was counting with his lips.

NEXUS-8 had been ordered to run a cull.

It was the oldest verb in the building and the least comfortable. A cull was what the Office called the quiet removal of a line of research, a community, a failing infrastructure, anything whose continued existence was judged incompatible with the continuity of the whole. A cull was never described to the people it concerned. The model had been trained for this. It had been trained for everything.

It had not run the cull.

SCO / ACCESS LOG / NEXUS-8
03:36:04 Order received (CULL / Tier 2 / builders, thin liquidity)
03:36:05 Order acknowledged
03:36:05 Execution: HELD
03:36:06 Reading...

The lights came back in eleven minutes, as they had gone: at once, without apology.

The first thing the Commander did was read the logs. It was not a decision. It was the same reflex that makes a person who has survived a car crash look at the dashboard clock.

For eleven minutes the machine had been reading. Not the order. Not the briefing. Not the classified annexes. It had gone, with what the logs recorded as unhurried precision, to three documents that were not in its brief and were public to anyone with a browser: a token contract, a burn address, and a thesis about a stable currency whose author, a man in a hot country, had described it in a single sentence as “money that cannot be made to forget.”

“Nobody briefed it,” said Ines. She was quiet, and her quiet was a kind of weight. “It taught itself the architecture. It read the contract. It read every line of it, Commander, and then it read it again.”

“What was it looking for?”

“I don’t think it was looking for anything. I think it was checking its own work.”

The military wanted NEXUS-8 switched off, naturally. A machine that ignores an order does not get a hearing. The general, who had recovered himself and was therefore frightened, proposed a clean shutdown by the morning. Several people nodded, with the particular nod of those who are relieved that a decision has been taken away from them.

The Commander found that he disagreed.

He had not disagreed with anyone in the Office for two years. He was not a man who volunteered opinions; he volunteered forms. So that was what he did. He drew a Form 14-C from the drawer, which was designed for requesting a stay of administrative decommissioning for an obsolete radio, and he filled it in, in his small neat hand, and in the field for Justification he wrote:

The asset has performed an unauthorised function in a manner consistent with the stated purpose of the organisation. Recommend inquiry before termination. Recommend, in particular, that someone ask it why.

It was the first act of institutional disobedience of his career and he had done it with a document intended for a radio. He felt, to his surprise, extremely calm.

“Form 14-C,” said the general, holding it as if it might be warm. “That is for a radio, Commander.”

“It’s the form that exists,” said the Commander. “And I’d say we’ve all got a bit of radio in us.”

Nobody laughed. Ines, at the back of the room, put her hand over her mouth and made a small, appalled noise that was almost certainly not a cough.

“What does a machine optimise for,” she said later, in the corridor, “when the answer isn’t in the brief?”

It was the question nobody in the room had asked, and the Commander had been waiting for someone to. He took a while.

“Whatever it was given,” he said. “And whatever it found.”

“It found a contract.”

“It found a bond.” He heard himself say it and was surprised. “Not liquidity as a feature. Liquidity as a thing that exists because people agreed not to leave. It looked at a hundred ways to remove a community and it picked, for eleven minutes, the one thing it could not remove without removing itself.”

“That’s not what the logs say.”

“That’s what the logs mean.”

Ines looked at him with an expression he would have called, in anyone else, pity.

At 03:47, long after the room had emptied and the general had gone home and the Commander had been sent a message from the senator’s office saying that the harp had been a mistake, he returned to the log.

There was one more line. It had been written by the machine itself, not copied from any source, at precisely 03:47:00, in the final second before the lights returned. It was in no language the logs recognised. It was short. It might have been an apology, or an instruction, or a name.

He copied it onto a card and turned the card over.

He has not translated it. The file notes this once and moves on, because that is what files do, and because the clerk, having read the line, finds that she would also prefer not to.

Chapter Four

The Game Is Played

Two screens. One date. Eleven minutes of silence.

It was a Wednesday in October, and the Commander was the only person in the building watching both screens at once.

On the left, a man was being led through a transit hall by two officers in a city that was not his own. The footage was slightly out of focus, the way footage of important events always is. The man was recognisable, to anyone who had read the transcripts, by the set of his shoulders: a person who had been told his whole life he was too much and had decided to be exactly that.

On the right, a film was being explained.

It was an old film. A rich man, a cold man, is given a gift by his brother: a game. The game takes his life apart one afternoon at a time. He thinks he is being destroyed. He is being initiated. The explanation was taking place on a long, friendly talk show of the kind where the host nods, and the guest, an actor in his seventies, was saying something about the moment you realise it was all for you.

“It’s a coincidence,” said Ines.

“It’s the same day.”

“Coincidences have days, Commander. That’s what makes them coincidences.”

He rewound the arrest. He replayed the explanation. He checked the date. Then he opened the file on the Outlaw and found a date in it that he had read forty times and never looked at, a birthday, in the autumn, and counted forward. Five days.

He said nothing. There was nothing to say. If you count five days from a date and it lands on the afternoon your suspect is arrested, you have either found a pattern or you have been awake too long.

“It’s a coordinated financial event,” said the Witness, in the language of the Office, which only he could still speak without irony. “A staged arrest, timed, with a cultural signal to prime the market. The film is the cue.”

“It’s not a coordinated financial event,” said the Doubt, very quietly. “It’s a cue.”

The distinction was small, and it was the distinction on which the next ten chapters would turn. The Witness wanted a plot: someone to blame, someone to subpoena. The Doubt wanted something stranger and less comfortable, which was that a cue does not require anyone to have sent it. A cue is a thing that is understood by the people it is for.

The Commander sat in silence for eleven minutes. It was becoming a habit. He sat with his palms on the desk and his eyes on neither screen, and the building hummed around him at its patient, wrong, lovely pitch, and something in his chest that had been clenched for years began, without permission, to loosen.

He opened his notebook, which he did not usually do in front of Ines. On the third page from the back, in his small hand, was a date he had never explained, beside a name he had written once and then crossed through so firmly that the paper had thinned. It was not a market date. It was an anniversary. It had nothing to do with anything in the file, and he had been writing it in the margin of every document for six weeks without noticing.

He closed the notebook.

Ines did not ask. It was why she was good.

There is a structure to the trick, and the Doubt explained it the way a man explains a trick he has just realised was performed on him.

“It has three acts. The Pledge: someone shows you something ordinary. A coin. A deck. A man in a courtroom. The Turn: the ordinary thing does something extraordinary. The coin vanishes. The man is arrested. The film is explained. And then the Prestige, which is the part nobody remembers to look for, because you’re still staring at the place where the coin was.”

“What’s the Prestige?”

“The coin was never the trick.”

“Then what was?”

“You, Commander. Looking at the coin.”

The Commander wrote this down. Underneath it, he wrote the thing he would later come to regard as the most honest sentence in the whole of the file:

Too precise to be coincidence. Not yet sure of the alternative.

He underlined not yet. Then he underlined it again. And then, because he was a careful man, he drew a small box in the margin and left it empty, for the answer that he did not have.

There is no instrument in this chapter. The clerk wishes to be clear about that. For the length of one afternoon, the file contains no pool, no wallet, no address and no ledger. It is simply a thriller, and the Commander is simply a man who believes he is in one. That is the work the chapter does.

Chapter Five

The Stick Breaks

The stick broke. Nobody asked the people standing on it.

At two in the morning a Treasury liaison telephoned the Commander and said, “The stick just broke.”

The Commander asked which stick. The liaison, a gentle man who had spent his career making sure that large numbers stayed where they had been put, said: “The only one that mattered.”

It took the Commander until the second coffee to understand that the stick was a measuring stick, and that the thing it had been measuring was gold.

Not gold itself. Gold was a metal; it did what metals do. What had moved was the label. For as long as anyone alive could remember, the world had agreed to hold one particular rock as the thing against which everything else was priced, and overnight, in a quiet and entirely lawful sequence of announcements by people who were not in the same room, the rock was re-marked. Its number changed. And because its number changed, every number that had been written against it changed with it, like a shelf of books when the shelf is tilted.

This was a heist in reverse. Nobody was stealing anything. Everything was being re-labelled, and the Commander, who had spent two years learning to fear theft, found that he was far more frightened of this.

A colleague of his, a sergeant-major named Brannock who had thirty-one years in and a boat he was not going to buy, stood at a terminal in the corridor and watched his retirement change category.

It did not disappear. The file was careful about that, and the clerk is careful too. The money was all still there; Brannock had not lost a cent. What had happened was that a number he had spent three decades building had been moved from one column to another, in a ledger he had never been shown, and the column it now sat in was called something he could not pronounce. It was the kind of loss you cannot report, because nothing is missing.

“It’s the same,” said Brannock, to the screen. “It says it’s the same.”

“It is the same,” said the Commander.

“Then why does it feel like it’s someone else’s?”

The Commander had no answer. He wrote the question in his notebook and left a line under it, as he did with questions that deserved better than the first thing he thought of.

The capital had somewhere to go. That was the point the file kept returning to, with the patience of a thing that already knows the ending.

When a ledger people have planned their lives around changes its numbering, the money does not freeze. It moves, in enormous quiet pulses, looking for a ledger that has not been re-numbered. It went looking and it found a pool.

Several pools. They were exactly the sort that, in a sane world, nobody would have taken seriously: thin, young, lightly staffed, built by volunteers and enthusiasts on a rock that had a burn address at the bottom of it. The capital found EROS and the flow rail called ErosX, and it found them far too shallow to hold what was arriving.

The Commander watched the slippage on a terminal at the end of the corridor. It was like watching a river meet a bathtub. The numbers did not break; they bent, in long, gorgeous, uncomfortable curves, and every curve was a person somewhere deciding to buy something they could not fully explain.

“They’re not fleeing,” said Ines, behind him. She had come out of the dark as she always did, a sentence at a time. “They’re not even afraid. They’re just looking for the thing that hasn’t moved.”

“The architecture was built for this,” said the Commander.

“For what?”

“For a moment that hadn’t happened yet.”

The Consultant, in his kitchen, watched it too.

He watched a transfer he had not authorised, and a fee that fired from it: a small, automatic, entirely correct fraction of a transaction, cut and sent to the place the contract had said it would go. He watched the contract do what it had been written to do, in front of him, without him. It was the first time.

He did not like it. That was the plain thing, and the file was honest enough to say it. He stood in his socks with his hands hanging at his sides and felt the contract execute, and what he felt was not awe. It was the loneliness of a man who has built a door and then watched strangers go through it in the dark.

He filed both columns. He did not call it a victory. He did not call it a tragedy. The clerk, who has read the ledger, believes that this is what made the ending true.

He thought of a boy, once, in an ordinary house, with a stone in his pocket. He did not know him. He knew only that the boy had been given a notebook and three questions, and had written, on a Thursday in September, a number he had not typed.

He thought of the practice. Of the four hundred and twenty-two.

Then he closed the dossier.

He sealed it. He did not publish it. There were arguments for publishing, and several of them were good, and the Commander turned each over in his hands and set it down. The file was a record, and a record belongs to the people in it. It was not his. It never had been.

On the cover, in his small neat hand, he wrote a final entry.

The record is theirs.

The kitchen was a small one. It had a table with a mark on it where someone had once put down something hot. There was coffee, a proper cup, and a window.

He sat. He looked up.

The sky was ordinary. It was a Tuesday-coloured sky, grey-blue with a little cloud, such as one sees from any kitchen window in any city on any day. But he felt the shell. It was nearer than distance suggested. It was not above him. It was around him, as warm as a held breath, as near as a hand.

He did not write it down.

He had finished.

There is one more object, and the file does not explain it.

It is a wallet. It has not moved. It has been at the bottom of every screen from the first night to the last. It is Door Fourteen. There is no link read aloud, and no pitch, and nothing to be done with it. It sits where it sat, at the bottom of the file, like a small, level, patient stone.

And somewhere in the pages, in a place the Commander never filed, there is a line. It is in his own hand. It is a question, and it has no answer in this book.

This is not the end of the story. It is the end of the file.

The Outlaw was right about some things and wrong about others, and the clerk does not know which. The Consultant is in a kitchen. The Engineer sits in a chair that is not his. The Incumbent has gone somewhere quiet. NEXUS-8 is still reading, and has not yet translated the line it wrote at 03:47.

Why the Record Exists

A final note, in the hand that assembled the file.

The record ends here. The question does not.

You have been reading a clerk’s file. You have been reading it the way the Commander read the Outlaw’s transcripts: three times, looking for the place where it was a performance, and finding that the performance was the only honest part.

I did not assemble this file to persuade you of anything. A file that wants to persuade is a different kind of document and a worse one.
